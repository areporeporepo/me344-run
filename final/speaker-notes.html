Slide 1: Title (0:00 to 0:45)

This is Train, Tune and Scale Qwen3-4B on a TPU v5e-8.

Forty minutes of wall clock on eight v5e chips, about five and a quarter chip-hours, answering three questions: what fits, what is worth running, and what breaks when you add more chips.

The short version of all three. What fits is tensor parallel, not data parallel, and I predicted that before running it. What is worth running is global batch 32, which is nowhere near the memory limit. And what breaks is not the chip count, it is the network fabric.

Everything I show you traces to a named artifact file from one run.

Advance.

Slide 2: Memory (0:45 to 1:45)

First: the useful batch and the memory wall are not the same shape, and that gap is the whole point of the capacity lab.

Throughput saturates at global batch 32. Sixteen thousand five hundred tokens a second, using 59% of HBM. The first shape that actually fails to compile is global batch 512. That is four doublings of headroom between "as fast as this gets" and "out of memory", and everything in between spends memory for nothing. Going from 32 to 64 buys you 0.4% more throughput for 13% more memory.

The other number here is the ceiling. 25.5% of the chip's BF16 peak, and it plateaus there across an eight-times range of batch sizes. More work per step does not help after gb32.

Why not? Not because the arithmetic units are saturated: at a quarter of peak they clearly are not. I left this open on the day, and I have since closed it. Running the identical config with FSDP instead of tensor parallel, on the same chip with the same memory footprint, more than doubles it, to 52.7%. Tensor parallelism all-reduces activations at every layer, so its traffic grows with token count and no batch size escapes it. The ceiling was my sharding choice, not the hardware.

[cut] And the cheapest thing on this slide is the ahead-of-time compile. Ten chip-minutes of compute told me the DP8 layout would die before I ran a single training step. It needed 65 gigabytes against 15.75 available.

Advance.

Slide 3: The model (1:45 to 2:45)

Second: fine-tuning worked, and the evidence for it is thinner than the headline number suggests.

Two hundred steps of SFT moved banking77 exact match from 70% to 92.5% on forty held-out prompts, with format compliance holding at 100% and GSM8K retention going up rather than down. That is a clean result and it is the one I would ship.

Here is why I will not call it a launch decision. GRPO's accuracy effect came out plus 12.5 points on this run, and minus 12.5 points on an identically configured run three days earlier, with the guardrail flipping from pass to fail. That is a two-example swing on a sixteen-item set.

The primary suite says the same thing more formally. The Wilson intervals still overlap by 1.8 points, and I would need 97 examples for a plus or minus ten point margin. I used forty.

So the honest framing is: this is a release smoke test, not a launch.

Advance.

Slide 4: Scaling (2:45 to 4:00)

Third, and this is the result I would actually defend: chip count is not the scaling unit. The fabric is.

Going from 8 to 16 chips inside one ICI slice is essentially free. 100.1% strong scaling efficiency, 99.6% weak. Per chip throughput goes from 2,025 tokens a second to 2,026. You get the second eight chips for nothing.

Now take those same sixteen chips and split them across two slices, so the data-parallel gradient all-reduce has to cross DCN instead of ICI. Same model, same batches, same logical layout. Efficiency drops to 65.2% strong and 53.6% weak.

And there is one result I cannot explain. DCN weak scaling came out worse per chip than DCN strong. 1,085 tokens per second per chip against 1,320. An all-reduce averages gradients, which is one value per parameter, so its payload is fixed by the model and does not grow when you double the batch. Weak scaling should not be worse than strong. Differencing against ICI at matched batch shows 3.51 seconds of exposed communication against 1.08, a 3.2 times jump for the same payload.

Lost compute-communication overlap, rematerialization at 110% of the AOT limit, and contention from other students on a shared fabric are all consistent with that. The GKE jobs saved no traces, so I genuinely cannot tell you which it is. Re-running those two trials with profiling on is the first thing I would do with more time.

Advance.

Slide 5: Outlook (4:00 to 5:00)

So: what I would ship, and what survives this hardware.

I would ship the SFT checkpoint at step 202. It is the only candidate with a domain gain, no retention regression, and an actual serving measurement behind it. Not GRPO, because its gain did not reproduce.

[cut] One caveat I will state rather than hide: the run's own stopping rule prefers step 50, where held-out loss was best at 0.0436 before drifting to 0.0623 by step 200. No task metric was ever run against step 50.

Now the part that outlives the assignment. This chart takes the 16 bytes per parameter I measured and runs it forward. Kimi K3, at 2.8 trillion parameters, needs 44.8 terabytes of training state. That is eleven times the HBM of an entire 256-chip v5e pod. An Ironwood pod clears it with room. That gap is why 2.8-trillion-parameter open models arrived in 2026 and not in 2023.

The fabric result ages best. On Ironwood the boundary moves from 256 chips to 9,216, but it does not disappear, and mixture-of-experts models add a second collective, a per-layer all-to-all. Google's new inference part drops the 3D torus for a topology that cuts network diameter from 16 hops to 7 for a claimed 50% better all-to-all latency, and names MoE serving as the reason. So they reached the same conclusion I did.

[cut] One caveat on my own numbers: the 99% matmul figure is a claim about sequence length 256 only. Attention scales as L squared against matmul's L, so at a million tokens the same step is 97% attention, which is why Kimi K3 and Qwen3.5 replaced most of their attention layers.

And a prediction to close on. Google says these chips were built to train and serve world models like Genie 3, so millions of agents can rehearse in simulation. If that is where the compute goes, the systems question of 2027 is not tokens per second on a training step, it is the throughput and latency of a simulate, act, learn loop, and the fabric question I measured reappears between the model and its environment.
