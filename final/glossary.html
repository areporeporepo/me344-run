Every Acronym In This Project, And How It Actually Works

Written for the presentation. Expanding the letters is not enough: if someone asks why ICI beat DCN, "inter-chip interconnect" is not an answer. So each entry says what it stands for, what it actually does, and where it shows up in your numbers.

Start with the twelve. If you only have an hour, learn those cold and skim the rest.

Hardware

TPU, Tensor Processing Unit. An ASIC built around a systolic array: a grid of multiply-accumulate units where data flows through and partial sums accumulate in place, so you avoid re-reading operands from memory for every operation. That is why TPUs are efficient at dense matmul and unremarkable at branchy code.

v5e, and v5litepod-8. The fifth-generation "efficiency" TPU, aimed at inference and cost-sensitive training. v5litepod-8 is eight of those chips on one host. The "lite" is real: v5e has no FP8, where the current Ironwood does.

HBM, High Bandwidth Memory. Stacked DRAM dies sitting on the same package as the compute die, connected by a very wide bus. You get enormous bandwidth and very little capacity, which is the central tension of this whole assignment. Your chip has 16 GB with 15.75 usable, and the run-time system reserves the difference.

SRAM / Vmem. On-chip scratchpad, much faster than HBM and much smaller. You never touched it directly, but it is why TPU 8i lists 384 MB as a headline spec: it is where KV cache lives during decoding.

2x4, 4x4, 3D torus. The physical wiring layout of a slice. Your 8 chips were a 2x4 grid. A torus wraps the edges around so every chip has neighbours in every direction, which keeps the worst-case hop count low. TPU 8i abandons the torus for a high-radix design that cuts the worst case from 16 hops to 7.

Slice, pod, superpod. A slice is a contiguous block of ICI-connected chips you get allocated (yours: 8). A pod is the largest ICI domain the hardware supports (v5e: 256 chips). A superpod is the marketing term for the biggest one on 8t: 9,600 chips.

FLOP, TFLOP, PFLOP. Floating-point operation. Tera is 10^12, Peta is 10^15. Watch the trailing /s: TFLOP is an amount of work, TFLOP/s is a rate. Your step is 24.94 TFLOPs of work, and you sustained 50.4 TFLOP/s.

BF16, FP8, FP4, INT8, INT4. Number formats, by bits. BF16 keeps FP32's 8-bit exponent so you rarely overflow, at the cost of precision. Fewer bits means more arithmetic per second and less memory per parameter, which is why TPU 8t leads with FP4 at 12.6 PFLOP/s.

MXFP4 / MXFP8. Microscaling floats. A block of values shares one scaling factor, so you get 4-bit or 8-bit storage without the dynamic range collapsing. Kimi K3 uses these.

PPU, Parallel Processing Unit. Alibaba's in-house accelerator architecture, as in the Zhenwu M890. A third lineage alongside NVIDIA and TPU.

SparseCore. A TPU co-processor for embedding lookups and sparse operations. It matters to you only because enabling its collective offloading changes the memory layout, and therefore moves where OOM happens.

Parallelism and collectives

This is the section that carries slide 4, so it is worth real time.

DP, Data Parallel. Every device gets a complete copy of the model and a different slice of the batch. Simple, but every device pays full memory cost. Your DP8 test needed 59.94 GiB of state per chip against 15.75 available, which is exactly why it died.

TP, Tensor Parallel. Split the weight matrices themselves across devices. Each chip stores 1/8 of every layer, so state per chip drops roughly 8x. The cost is communication inside every layer, which is only tolerable over a fast fabric. This is why TP lives inside an ICI slice and not across DCN.

EP, Expert Parallel. MoE-specific. Different experts live on different devices, so tokens must be routed to wherever their expert is, then routed back. This is the all-to-all that dominates MoE performance.

DP2 x TP8. Your GKE 16-chip layout. Two data-parallel replicas, each internally split 8 ways tensor-parallel. The point of the experiment: keep this layout identical and change only whether the DP dimension crosses ICI or DCN.

Collective operations. Communication patterns where all participants take part:

  • all-reduce: everyone contributes a value, everyone receives the combined result. Gradient averaging in data parallelism. Payload size is fixed by the model, not the batch, which is why your DCN weak-scaling result is anomalous.
  • all-gather: everyone ends up with everyone's piece.
  • all-to-all: every device sends a different chunk to every other device. This is MoE expert routing, it is latency-sensitive, and it is why TPU 8i got a new topology.

Strong vs weak scaling. Strong: fix total work, add chips, hope it finishes faster. Weak: grow the work with the chips, hope time-per-step stays flat. You measured both. ICI held ~100% on each; DCN gave 65.2% strong and 53.6% weak.

Exposed communication. Communication time that is not hidden behind compute. Good systems overlap the two so the network works while the chips compute. Your DCN run had 3.51 s exposed against ICI's 1.08 s, and that 3.2x gap for an unchanged payload is the thing you cannot explain.

Remat / rematerialization (also "gradient checkpointing"). Instead of storing every intermediate activation for the backward pass, throw some away and recompute them. Trades compute for memory. This is why your gb256 config ran despite AOT predicting 110% of the memory limit, and it is what MaxText's estimator.py searches over.

Pathways. Google's orchestration layer that lets one JAX program drive chips across many pods. It is what makes the "over 1 million chips in a single training cluster" claim possible.

Compilation

XLA, Accelerated Linear Algebra. The compiler underneath JAX and TPU. It takes your whole computation graph and optimizes it as a unit: fusing operations, choosing layouts, scheduling communication. You do not write TPU kernels; XLA emits them.

HLO, High Level Operations. XLA's intermediate representation. When an error mentions "HLO temporaries", it means scratch buffers XLA allocated for intermediate results. Your DP8 failure was 65.47 GB of HLO temporaries.

JIT, Just-In-Time. Compile on first call, at runtime. The default in JAX. It is why the first training step takes ~18 seconds and later ones take 4.

AOT, Ahead-Of-Time. Compile without executing, on hardware you do not have. Three properties make it valuable: it reports the same OOM you would hit for real, you can run it on a CPU while targeting a TPU cluster, and the compiled artifact can be saved and reloaded. This is the technique that turned out to scale furthest in your whole project.

Compiled signature. The shapes and configuration baked into a compiled artifact. Change batch size or sequence length and it will not match; you get a clear error rather than silent misbehavior.

Software stack

JAX. The Python array framework you used. Its bargain: write pure functions, get automatic differentiation, vectorization and compilation. jax.jit compiles, jax.grad differentiates.

MaxText. Google's reference JAX LLM implementation, pinned in your run at commit 17c7172. Provides the training loop, sharding and model definitions. train_compile.py is its AOT entry point.

XProf. The TPU profiler. Captures a trace of what every core did per step, which is how you got the 99.07% matmul against 0.93% attention split. Viewed through TensorBoard.

W&B / wandb, Weights and Biases. Experiment tracking. You ran it in offline mode, so metrics were written to disk rather than uploaded.

GCS, Google Cloud Storage. Object storage. Every gs:// path. Where your checkpoints, scaling results and submission archive live.

GKE, Google Kubernetes Engine. Managed Kubernetes. Ran your three 16-chip scaling jobs.

JobSet. A Kubernetes extension for jobs that need several coordinated pods, which is what a multi-worker TPU job is. Your scale-job.yaml defines one.

kubectl. The Kubernetes CLI. kubectl get pods, kubectl logs.

uv, venv. Fast Python package installer, and virtual environments. You had two: one for pre-training, one for post-training, because their dependencies conflict.

Training and evaluation

Checkpoint. A saved snapshot of model weights plus optimizer state. Yours are at steps 200 and 202. "Step 202" exists because you resumed and did two more steps to prove resumption worked.

Optimizer state. What the optimizer remembers between steps. Adam keeps two extra values per parameter (momentum and variance), which is most of why training costs 16 bytes per parameter rather than the 2 bytes the BF16 weights alone would need.

SFT, Supervised Fine-Tuning. Train on (prompt, correct answer) pairs with ordinary cross-entropy loss. Predictable and effective. 200 steps took you from 70.0% to 92.5%.

RL, Reinforcement Learning. Learning from a reward signal rather than labelled answers.

GRPO, Group Relative Policy Optimization. Sample a group of answers for one prompt, score them, and push the model toward the ones that scored above the group's average. The "relative" part removes the need for a separate value-estimating model, which is what makes it cheap enough to run in a class assignment. It moved your format compliance from 37.5% to 93.75%.

Reward / guardrail. The score GRPO optimizes, plus a check that it did not wreck something else while chasing it. Your guardrail passed this run and failed the previous one.

Cross-entropy loss, perplexity. Loss measures surprise at the correct token. Perplexity is exp(loss), readable as "effectively how many options was the model choosing between". Your held-out loss was 0.0436 at its best.

Held-out set. Examples never trained on. The only honest measure of whether it learned rather than memorized. Yours was 40 prompts.

Exact match, format compliance. Two different questions. Exact match: was the answer right? Format compliance: was it even shaped like an answer? GRPO mostly fixed the second.

banking77. A 2020 dataset of 13k retail-banking customer messages labelled with 77 intents. Your SFT task. Genuinely uninteresting as a benchmark, and deliberately so: it is a fixture chosen so a small model changes measurably in 200 steps.

GSM8K. Grade School Math 8K, a word-problem benchmark. Used here as a retention check: did teaching it banking intents damage its arithmetic? It went 81.25% to 87.5%, so no.

Wilson interval. A confidence interval for a proportion that behaves properly with small samples, where the naive formula does not. Yours: 80.1% to 97.4% for SFT against 54.6% to 81.9% for base. They overlap, which is the entire reason you call your result a smoke test.

Early stopping. Stop when held-out loss stops improving rather than when the step budget runs out. Your best was step 50, and by step 200 it had drifted from 0.0436 to 0.0623.

Architecture, the 2026 frontier

MoE, Mixture of Experts. Replace one big feed-forward block with many "experts" and a router that sends each token to only a few. Kimi K3 activates 16 of 896. Total parameters explode while per-token compute stays modest, so HBM capacity binds before FLOPs do, and expert routing adds an all-to-all per layer.

Router, active vs total parameters, sparsity. The router picks experts per token. Qwen3-30B-A3B means 30B total, 3B active. That ratio is the sparsity, and it has been rising steadily.

Attention, and why L matters. Attention lets each token look at every other token, so its cost grows as L^2 while the weight matmuls grow as L. At your L=256 attention was 0.93% of the step. At 1M tokens the same arithmetic makes it ~97%.

KV cache. During generation, the keys and values for previous tokens are cached instead of recomputed. At long context this becomes the dominant memory consumer, which is why TPU 8i advertises 384 MB of on-chip SRAM.

MLA, Multi-head Latent Attention. Compresses the KV cache into a smaller latent representation. Used by Kimi K2.

KDA, Kimi Delta Attention. Kimi K3's hybrid: mostly linear-attention layers, with a full attention layer every fourth one. Reported 6.3x faster decoding at 1M context.

Gated DeltaNet. Qwen3.5's equivalent, replacing 75% of standard attention layers.

Linear attention. Any attention variant with cost linear in L instead of quadratic, bought by approximating the all-pairs comparison. This is the direct response to the L^2 problem above.

World model. A model that generates an environment you can act inside, rather than text. Google's Genie 3 makes navigable worlds at 24 fps. Google says TPU 8t and 8i were built to train and serve these, so agents can rehearse in simulation.

Drill: questions you should be able to answer without notes

  1. Why did DP8 fail when TP8 fit? DP replicates all 16 bytes/parameter of training state on every chip; TP shards it 8 ways. 59.94 GiB against 15.75 available.
  2. Why is ICI faster than DCN, physically? ICI is dedicated links inside a slice. DCN is general datacenter networking between slices, with far less bandwidth and much higher latency.
  3. Why is the DCN weak-scaling result strange? An all-reduce payload is set by parameter count, not batch size. Doubling the batch should not make per-chip throughput worse. It did: 1,085 against 1,320.
  4. Why does throughput plateau at gb32 when memory keeps growing? Something other than work per step is the limit past that point. You did not isolate what, and saying so is better than guessing.
  5. Why is 99.07% matmul not a general fact? Because attention scales as L^2 and matmul as L. It is a statement about L=256.
  6. Why not ship GRPO? Its accuracy gain was two examples on a 16-item set and it flipped sign on an identical earlier run.
  7. What does 16 bytes per parameter consist of? Roughly 12 persistent (weights plus Adam's two moments, in mixed precision) plus 4 transient at peak.
  8. What would you do next? Re-run the two DCN trials with XProf on, because that anomaly is the only broken measurement in the project and it costs 20 minutes.
